Research

Working paper

Clean Fuel, Gender, and the Allocation of Time: Evidence from Spousal Time Diaries in Bangladesh

With Atonu Rabbani. Working paper, October 2026. Based on my MSS thesis, University of Dhaka, 2026.

In Bangladeshi households that cook with traditional fuels, a wife spends about three and a half hours a day on cooking and collecting fuel. Her husband spends about four minutes. This paper asks whose time clean cooking fuel saves, what the saving is made of, and which households gain the most.

We use the 2021 Bangladesh Time Use Survey to compare 2,935 wives with their own husbands, who live in the same household and eat from the same stove. A collective model of the household with Nash bargaining guides the analysis.

Clean fuel is associated with about 26 fewer minutes a day of wives’ cooking and fuel-collection time, around 12.5 per cent of what they would otherwise spend. Husbands’ time does not change. The saving comes from fuel collection, which nearly disappears, and not from faster cooking: time spent preparing meals is almost the same with either fuel.

Poorer households collect more fuel to begin with, so their wives gain the most. More men take part in cooking in clean-fuel households, but those who cook do not cook for longer. The time wives get back moves out of other household chores and into unpaid care.

These estimates are adjusted associations, not causal effects. They suggest that evaluations of clean cooking that count only health benefits leave out a substantial gain, and that reliable fuel delivery and support aimed at poorer households may matter as much as stove efficiency.

Accepted for presentation at the 5th East West Economics Research Conclave, East West University, Dhaka, 27–28 November 2026.

Writing for a general audience

Sundarbans: A Critical Pillar for Climate, Economic Resilience in South Asia

With Milan Mathew and Raktimava Bose. Opinion article, The Daily Guardian, 30 November 2024.

The Sundarbans protects millions of people in India and Bangladesh from cyclones and supports their livelihoods. We argue that rising seas, nearby industry and pressure on its resources call for the two countries to protect the forest together.
